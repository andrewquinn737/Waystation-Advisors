# 03. Selling a category the buyer has never heard of

Scope: both offers are unfamiliar to owners. Working Signal is a brand-new product category ("someone will pay me for my records?"). Waystation is a firm with no household name asking about the most personal decision an owner makes. Labeling: items with a source URL are **Research base (lower weight than our own call data)** unless stated otherwise.

## The core problem

Owners have no mental slot for "sell my job logs to AI companies." They will fill the gap with the closest familiar thing: a scam, a telemarketer, or a data-theft attempt. So the call has two jobs: (1) fit the idea into a slot they already understand, and (2) lower the perceived risk of saying yes to the next step.

## 1. Problem-first, not product-first

Research base: Armand Farrokh's framework replaces the product pitch with a "problem proposition," a short statement of a problem the owner probably recognizes, with a question. Source: https://echai.ventures/gtm/r/the-ultimate-30mpc-cold-calling-framework--r-pip2-22 . Jason Bay advises reps who lack brand recognition to name the specific challenges of the prospect, mirroring pain points from research, and to prove relevance with one fact from the prospect's world. Source: https://www.pulse.bot/b2b-growth/video/nail-the-problem-when-you-lack-brand-notoriety-4bfdddea-98a0-4dfb-bd61-1a351b4c9099/ (auto-generated summary page, check against the original podcast).

Our version has to be honest. These owners often do not have a "pain." The "problem" is an overlooked asset, not a failure. Frame it as an unrealized opportunity, not a problem you made up.

Working Signal examples:
- "A lot of shops have years of drawings and job records sitting in filing cabinets or old servers that never earn anything. AI companies are now paying for that kind of real-world operating data. Is that something you'd even know the value of?"
- "Do you ever get asked about those records by anyone, outside of an audit?"

Waystation examples:
- "Most owners we talk to have built something real, but nobody has told them what a buyer would pay for it, and they don't want to be listed on a broker site to find out. We have a buyer who'd rather talk privately first. Does that sound like something you'd be open to hearing?"

## 2. Credibility without a track record

We cannot cite deals, customers, or case studies that are not documented. Do not borrow credibility we do not have. Use these honest substitutes:

- **Specificity.** Naming the right trade, the right town, and a real detail from their website shows you did homework. It outperforms a vague claim of experience. Research base: Bay: "they're calling people that they know are a good fit" and add one relevant fact. Source: https://predictablerevenue.com/?p=130866
- **Transparency about the cold call.** Saying "I know I'm calling out of the blue" and "you can hang up if it's not relevant" is its own credibility signal. Gong's top performing opener type owns the cold call. Source: https://www.gong.io/blog/the-best-and-worst-cold-call-openers-backed-by-data-from-300m-calls
- **Verifiable identity.** Website, a real person's name, an office line they can call back, a written follow-up. Offer these before they ask.
- **No-money, no-sensitive-info start.** State what you are NOT asking for (no payment from them, no passwords, no confidential files).
- **A real buyer (Waystation).** The buyer's existence is the credential, but only state what the approved brief says about them. Never imply deal volume.

## 3. Curiosity

Research base: Josh Braun's "poke the bear" uses neutral, curiosity-driven questions that open dialogue without pressure, e.g. "I often hear that orgs your size face X. How does that compare with your experience?" Source: https://www.11x.ai/guides/josh-braun-cold-outreach-method

Adapted questions:
- "Out of curiosity, do you keep your old job records, or do you clear them out after a few years?"
- "When was the last time you looked at what your drawings archive might be worth to someone outside your shop?"
- "If a buyer wanted a business like yours quietly, have you ever thought about what you'd want them to know about you?"

Curiosity beats claims for a new category. A question the owner has to think about is better than a feature list they will reject.

## 4. Reducing perceived risk

New categories feel risky because the owner cannot predict what happens next. Fix it by describing the next step in small, concrete, reversible terms.

- Say what happens after "yes": "A 15-minute call with [name], no paperwork, and you can stop any time."
- Say what you will not ask for.
- Use Sandler-style negative reverse selling: invite the "no" ("if it doesn't sound right, tell me and I'll cross you off"). It reduces pressure and speeds up honest answers. Source: https://blog.hubspot.com/sales/negative-reverse-selling
- Offer written details before any commitment.

## 5. Social proof substitutes (use only what is true)

Without testimonials, use:
- **Category logic:** "AI companies need real-world operating records and have run out of public data." Only say this if we can back it with a source on request. Do not make up statistics.
- **Peer adjacency:** "We're talking to other [trade] shops in [state]" only if true and permitted.
- **Process proof:** a written agreement template or a public website with a real person's name.
- **Buyer proof (Waystation):** the buyer's stated criteria from the brief.

Do not invent customer names, volumes, or "several of your competitors already sold" language. See the evidence guardrail in the outreach skills.

## 6. The ask: smallest possible next step

For a new category, the ask should be small enough that the owner says yes without understanding everything.

Research base: Gong reports that an "interest" CTA outperforms a specific date and time CTA on cold calls ("Does it make sense for me to give you more detail about how we do that?"). Source: https://www.gong.io/blog/cold-call-objections . Nooks reps who lead with value in the meeting ask (an expert, a data point) saw gains, but this is small-sample vendor data. Source: https://www.nooks.ai/blog-posts/how-the-top-1-of-reps-run-outbound-calls

Ask ladder (smallest first):
1. Permission to explain in 30 seconds.
2. One qualifying question (do you keep records / are you open to hearing about a buyer).
3. Permission to send a one-pager or short email.
4. A 15-minute intro call with the specialist (the real goal).

Never jump from step 1 to a long meeting. If the owner is lukewarm, take step 3 and set a follow-up.

## 7. A simple structure

1. Context and honesty ("out of the blue, here's why").
2. One-sentence what it is, in their language.
3. A curiosity question about their situation.
4. If they engage, a risk-reducer ("no cost, no confidential info, written first").
5. Small next step.

## Words to avoid

AI jargon ("training corpora," "foundation models"), "data licensing," "exit strategy," "M&A." Use the owner's language: "records," "job tickets," "drawings," "buyer," "talk about your business." Plain, short words. Confirm any terminology with Mason's data sale copy rules before it goes in a script.
